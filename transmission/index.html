On AFM trucks, the transmission is told to slip on purpose every time the engine drops to 4 cylinders. Here is how that works, what GM's own documents say about it, and how to stop it.

Steady highway miles. This is exactly when an AFM truck drops to 4 cylinders.

If you own a 2007 or newer GM truck or SUV, you may have heard it at a shop. The transmission fluid is dark. It smells burnt. The tech asks if you tow a lot. You say no, and the answer you get back is some version of "these just do that."

That answer skips the real question. Burnt fluid comes from heat, and heat in a transmission comes mostly from slip. So the question is what is making your transmission slip on a truck that does easy highway miles. On an AFM truck, the answer is built into the way the engine and transmission are programmed to work together.

Is this about your truck?

If you drive a 2007 or newer GM truck or SUV with a V8, and on many 2014 and newer trucks the V6 too, your engine very likely has AFM or DFM. That includes the Silverado, Sierra, Tahoe, Yukon, Suburban and Escalade.

Two ways to know for sure. On many of these trucks the info screen in the gauge cluster shows when it happens, as a V4 or ECO message depending on the year and trim. Or run your make, model and year through the fit check further down this page.

What AFM actually does inside your engine

Starting with the 2007 trucks, GM put a system called Active Fuel Management (AFM) on its V8s, and later on many V6s. On 2019 and newer trucks the updated version is called Dynamic Fuel Management (DFM). The idea is simple. When you are cruising and the engine does not need all its power, it shuts some cylinders off to save fuel.

Here is how it does that. Each cylinder has valves that let air in and exhaust out, and those valves are pushed open by lifters riding on the camshaft. On the cylinders AFM can shut off, the lifters are special. Each one has a small pin inside that can unlock. When the truck's computer decides you are cruising light, it sends engine oil pressure through a solenoid block in the top of the engine to those lifters. The pins unlock, the lifters collapse, and the valves on those cylinders stop opening. Fuel to those cylinders is cut. On an AFM V8 the shut off cylinders are 1, 4, 6 and 7, and the engine is now running as a V4.

The moment you ask for power, the oil pressure is released, the pins lock again and the engine is back to a V8 in a fraction of a second. On a normal drive that switch can happen dozens of times. DFM trucks can shut off different combinations of cylinders, so they switch even more often.

What all of that buys you is a small fuel saving, usually around 1 mpg. What it costs is a list of side effects that GM itself has documented in service bulletins. The one on this page is one of them.

How 4 cylinder mode reaches your transmission

Between your engine and your transmission sits the torque converter. At low speed it lets the engine spin a little faster than the transmission, which is what lets you sit at a red light in drive. At cruising speed, a clutch inside the converter, called the torque converter clutch, locks the two together so nothing slips. That lockup is what keeps a transmission cool on the highway. A locked converter makes almost no heat.

Now add AFM. When the engine drops to 4 cylinders, it fires half as often, and each firing has to push harder. That makes a deeper, lower shake. With the converter clutch fully locked, that shake goes straight through the driveline and into the cab. So GM's software does something about it: it lets the converter clutch slip on purpose while the engine is in 4 cylinder mode. A small, controlled slip soaks up the shake before you feel it.

This is not a guess. A GM patent on this exact strategy, titled "Active fuel management mode torque converter clutch control," describes commanding the converter clutch to slip before 4 cylinder mode turns on and adjusting that slip while the engine stays in it.

Steady speed, light throttle. The conditions AFM is designed for.

What controlled slip does to your fluid

A slipping clutch is two surfaces rubbing against each other through a thin film of fluid. Rubbing makes heat, and that heat goes straight into the transmission fluid. Heat breaks fluid down. It oxidizes, turns dark, loses the friction additives that let the clutch grab smoothly, and starts to smell burnt.

And it happens on the easiest driving your truck does. The engine drops to 4 cylinders at a steady cruise with light throttle, which is exactly what a highway commute is. A truck that never tows can spend hours a week with its converter clutch slipping, simply because it is being driven gently.

It also feeds on itself. GM's own diagnostic guidance for converter clutch shudder says it is most often caused by degraded clutch friction material and degraded fluid. Worn fluid makes the clutch grab unevenly, which causes shudder, and the usual answer to shudder is more slip. More slip means more heat, and more heat means more worn fluid.

Why a flush does not fix it

A flush puts fresh fluid into the same system. The software still drops the engine to 4 cylinders on the next highway drive, the converter clutch still slips on purpose, and the new fluid starts taking the same heat on day one. The fluid was never the cause. It was the evidence.

Before AFM, GM trucks did not shut cylinders off, so there was no reason to let the converter slip at cruise. That is a big part of why older GM trucks with high miles often have fluid that still looks like fluid.

A flush puts fresh fluid back into the same heat source.

Where it goes if nothing changes

The first sign is fluid that darkens quickly after a change. The next is a shudder, usually felt as a rumble strip feeling at light throttle somewhere around 30 to 50 mph, right when the converter is trying to lock. After that come soft or slipping shifts. By then the conversation at the shop is about a torque converter or a rebuild, and that is a bill in the thousands.
