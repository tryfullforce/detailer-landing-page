Is this about your truck?

If you drive a 2007 or newer GM truck or SUV with a V8, and on many 2014 and newer trucks the V6 too, your engine very likely has AFM or DFM. That includes the Silverado, Sierra, Tahoe, Yukon, Suburban and Escalade.

Two ways to know for sure. On many of these trucks the info screen in the gauge cluster shows when it happens, as a V4 or ECO message depending on the year and trim. Or run your make, model and year through the fit check further down this page.

What AFM actually does inside your engine

Starting with the 2007 trucks, GM put a system called Active Fuel Management (AFM) on its V8s, and later on many V6s. On 2019 and newer trucks the updated version is called Dynamic Fuel Management (DFM). The idea is simple. When you are cruising and the engine does not need all its power, it shuts some cylinders off to save fuel.

Here is how it does that. Each cylinder has valves that let air in and exhaust out, and those valves are pushed open by lifters riding on the camshaft. On the cylinders AFM can shut off, the lifters are special. Each one has a small pin inside that can unlock. When the truck's computer decides you are cruising light, it sends engine oil pressure through a solenoid block in the top of the engine to those lifters. The pins unlock, the lifters collapse, and the valves on those cylinders stop opening. Fuel to those cylinders is cut. On an AFM V8 the shut off cylinders are 1, 4, 6 and 7, and the engine is now running as a V4.

The moment you ask for power, the oil pressure is released, the pins lock again and the engine is back to a V8 in a fraction of a second. On a normal drive that switch can happen dozens of times. DFM trucks can shut off different combinations of cylinders, so they switch even more often.

What all of that buys you is a small fuel saving, usually around 1 mpg. What it costs is a list of side effects that GM itself has documented in service bulletins. The one on this page is one of them.

Number 7 is the back cylinder on the driver side.

Why it is always 1 and 7

GM V8s number their cylinders with odd numbers on the driver side, 1, 3, 5, 7 from front to back, and even numbers on the passenger side. AFM shuts off 1, 4, 6 and 7. That means on the driver side, the two cylinders AFM shuts off are 1 and 7. Those are exactly the two cylinders GM's own bulletin names.

GM service bulletin 10-06-01-008 covers excessive oil consumption on AFM V8s and lists cracked or fouled spark plugs on cylinder 1 and/or 7 as part of the condition. It names two ways the extra oil gets in: oil pulled into the intake through the PCV system, and oil sprayed from the AFM pressure relief valve inside the oil pan. The extra oil builds deposits in the piston ring grooves, the rings stop sealing, and oil gets into the cylinder.

The plug is the clue. The cause is upstream of it.

What GM changed

The bulletin's repair includes an updated driver side valve cover with a better PCV baffle, the cover on the same side as cylinders 1 and 7, plus cleaning the pistons and installing a deflector over the AFM pressure relief valve. GM notes trucks built before February 2011 did not have the updated cover from the factory. That is why this shows up most on 2007 to 2011 trucks.

Why the shut off cylinders take the worst of it

When AFM shuts a cylinder off, its valves close but the piston keeps moving. Every downward stroke in that sealed cylinder pulls a strong vacuum, like pulling back the plunger on a capped syringe. That vacuum pulls on the oil film below the rings, the same rings GM says can already be struggling with deposits. A firing cylinder burns off a little oil and keeps its plug fairly clean. A cylinder that spends its highway miles shut off and pulling vacuum does not, so the oil collects on the plug instead.

Put the two together and the pattern makes sense. Cylinders 1 and 7 are on the side with the PCV path GM had to fix, and both are cylinders AFM shuts off. Number 7, at the back, is where owners see it most.

A wet, oily tip next to dry ones points to oil entering that cylinder.

Why a new plug or an oil change does not fix it

A new plug goes into a cylinder that is still pulling oil, so it gets wet again. Shorter oil change intervals and better filters do not change the path the oil takes. Owners with perfect service records see this too, because it is not coming from neglect.

Left alone, an oily plug misfires. You feel it as a stumble at idle or a rough start, and a misfire sends unburned fuel and oil into the catalytic converters. The oil that got past the plug is also oil that is no longer in the pan, and on an AFM engine the cylinder shut off lifters run on that oil.

A perfect service record does not change it.
