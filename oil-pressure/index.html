I tune GM trucks on a chassis dyno. Every 5.3 and 6.2 that rolls onto the rollers gets a laptop hooked to it, and I log what I can: RPM, load, temps, and oil pressure. Twenty two years of that teaches you to trust the numbers over the forum threads.

A customer sent me a link to a patent filing. GM had applied for a redesigned oil path for its cylinder deactivation system. It was published in December 2025 as US 2025/0382903.

I read it twice. I am not a patent attorney, and I will not pretend to know what is going on inside GM. The filing describes routing the switching oil through the rocker shaft on future engines. It says nothing about the AFM and DFM engines already sitting in your driveway.

But a tuner reads a filing like that and thinks one thing. Nobody redraws an oil path for fun. So I went back to the logs on the trucks we already have in the shop.

What the logs showed

On my own truck, with under 30,000 miles on it, I watched oil pressure sag at low RPM. That was not a tired pump or a worn bottom end. That was a young engine doing exactly what it was set up to do.

Since 2014, the Gen V 5.3 and 6.2 have run a variable displacement oil pump. It backs pressure off at low RPM to save a little fuel. Fair trade for the window sticker. But it also leaves the AFM and DFM lifters with less oil behind them in the same low RPM window where the system is switching.

The number that matters is 22

Every AFM and DFM lifter carries a locking pin. To release that pin, the lifter needs roughly 22 psi behind it. On the trucks I log, the pump sits around 35 to 40 psi in the low RPM zone.

Figures from the author’s own dyno logs. One shop’s readings, not a factory spec. Your truck will differ.

That leaves about 13 psi of cushion at the low end. New engine, fresh oil, it works. But oil thins with heat and hours, and clearances open with miles. Both pull pressure down. Neither one pushes it up. That margin never gets wider. It only gets tighter.

When a lifter does not get what it needs, the pin can hang. A hung lifter collapses, it wipes out the cam lobe, and now you are pulling the heads. I have seen the invoices. They are not small.

What it does is stop the deactivation command. If the truck never asks a lifter to release its pin, no pin is riding on that thin margin in the first place.

What is going on inside your 5.3 or 6.2

AFM on the older trucks, DFM on the newer ones. Same idea, same little pin.

Your V8 runs on four

At steady cruise, GM’s Active Fuel Management (AFM, called DFM on newer trucks) shuts down cylinders to squeeze out a little fuel.

One pin per lifter does the switching

The lifters on those cylinders carry a small locking pin. Oil pressure releases it, and every switch is a pin cycle. On a normal drive that is hundreds of them.

When a pin hangs, the lifter goes

A hung pin can collapse the lifter and start eating the cam lobe. Plenty of these engines get through it. Some don’t, and nobody can tell you ahead of time which.

Example shown for a V8. V6 versions work the same way with fewer cylinders.

The pin does not count miles. It counts switches, and how much oil pressure is behind it each time.

Check your own truck this weekend

You do not need a shop for a first look. A basic Bluetooth reader runs about $15, and if you wrench at all you probably own one already.

  • Plug in your reader and open a scan app with live data. Any app that shows a live PID list will do.
  • Look for engine oil pressure. Not every truck or app reports it. If yours does not, thread a mechanical gauge into the oil pressure sender port. A shop can do it in minutes, or you can do it yourself.
  • Log two numbers. One at cold idle, one at warm idle. Write them down.
  • Compare the warm number to that 22 psi line. If it sits close, now you know something about your truck that most owners never find out.

This is a look, not a diagnosis. Readings vary by truck, oil grade, temperature and wear. If your truck is ticking, shuddering or losing power, get it on a lift before you buy anything.
